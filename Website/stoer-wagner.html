Overview

The Stoer-Wagner algorithm, created by Mechthild Stoer and Frank Wagner in 1994, solves the global minimum cut problem deterministically and without computing maximum flow. It takes an undirected graph G = (V, E) with n = |V| vertices and a nonnegative edge weight w(e) on every edge. An undirected graph is the case where every edge weight is 1, and a multigraph can be collapsed into this form by summing parallel edges.

Intuition

The algorithm runs |V| - 1 short phases, each of which produces one candidate cut, and then merges two vertices to shrink the graph. The lightest candidate over all phases is the minimum cut.

The foundational logic for this approach is that for any two vertices s and t, the true global minimum cut must either separate s and t, or it must place them on the same side. By finding the minimum cut separating a specific s and t (the cut of the phase), we evaluate the first scenario. If that cut is not the global minimum, s and t must belong to the same side of the global minimum cut, meaning we can safely merge them into a single vertex without destroying the true minimum cut.

Minimum Cut Phase

For a set of vertices X and a vertex v ∉ X, let w(X, v) be the total weight of the edges between X and v. The algorithm runs as follows:

  1. Choose an arbitrary starting vertex and let X be the set containing only that vertex.
  2. While X ≠ V, add to X the vertex v ∉ X that maximises w(X, v). Let t be the last vertex added and s the vertex added just before it.
  3. The cut of the phase is ({t}, V \ {t}), with value w(V \ {t}, t).
  4. Merge s and t into a single vertex. Edges from s and t to the same remaining vertex are replaced by one edge with the sum of their weights, and edges joining s and t are removed.

Run the minimum cut phase on the input graph and then on each shrunken graph until one vertex remains, giving n − 1 phases. Keep the lightest cut of the phase seen, and return it with each merged vertex expanded back to the original vertices it represents.

Pseudocode

MinimumCutPhase(G, a):
      A <- {a}
      while A != V:
        add to A the most tightly connected vertex
      return s, t, and the cut weight as the "cut of the phase"

      MinimumCut(G):
        minimum cut = nil
        while |V| > 1:
          choose any a from V
          cut of the phase = MinimumCutPhase(G, a)
          if the "cut of the phase" cut is lighter than the current minimum cut:
            minimum cut = cut of the phase
          shrink G by merging the two vertices from the "cut of the phase" (s, t)
        return the minimum cut

Worked Example

Proof of Correctness

Let s and t be the last two vertices added in a phase, with t added last. Let w(X, Y) denote the total weight of the edges between two sets of vertices X and Y.

To prove the overall correctness of the algorithm, we must prove the following two claims.

Claim 1: The cut of the phase, ({t}, V \ {t}), is a minimum s-t cut.

Proof. Let C denote any valid s-t cut. We must show that the cut of the phase is no heavier than C (i.e., w(V \ {t}, t) ≤ w(C)).

We define a vertex v as active if it is on the opposite side of C from the vertex added just before it. Because s and t are on opposite sides of C and s was added just before t, the vertex t is guaranteed to be active.

For an active vertex v, let Xv denote the set of vertices added before v, and let Cv be the edges of C that lie within Xv ∪ {v}. We claim that for every active vertex v,

w(Xv, v) ≤ w(Cv).

We prove this by induction through the active vertices in the order they were added.

Base Case: First active vertex v

Since no earlier vertex is active, all of Xv must lie on the same side of C, while v is on the opposite side. Therefore, every edge between Xv and v crosses C. Thus, the two sides of the claim are exactly equal, and the condition holds.

Inductive Step: Next active vertex u after an active vertex v

We partition the edges joining u to the earlier vertices into two distinct groups:

  • Edges from Xv: When v was chosen, it was the most tightly connected vertex to Xv, and u was a valid candidate. Hence, it holds that w(Xv, u) ≤ w(Xv, v). By our hypothesis for the previous active vertex v, w(Xv, v) ≤ w(Cv).
  • Edges from vertices added from v up to just before u: None of these vertices are active except v, meaning they all reside on the same side as v. Since u is active, it is on the opposite side. Therefore, all these edges cross C, and they represent new edges of C that were strictly not counted in Cv.

Adding these two groups together yields w(Xu, u) ≤ w(Cu).

Finally, since t is an active vertex and Xt ∪ {t} = V is the entire vertex set, it follows that Ct = C. Applying our claim yields w(V \ {t}, t) ≤ w(C). As this holds for any valid s–t cut C, the cut of the phase is guaranteed to be a minimum s–t cut.

Claim 2: The smallest of the n - 1 cuts of the phase is a minimum cut of the input graph.

Proof. Let k denote the weight of a minimum cut. We proceed by induction on the number of vertices.

For a graph with exactly two vertices, there is only one valid cut, which corresponds to the cut of its only phase, so the claim trivially holds.

For a larger graph, consider the first phase, with s and t as the last two vertices added. We must consider the following two cases:

Case 1: Some minimum cut separates s from t

This specific minimum cut is an s-t cut. As proven in Claim 1, the cut of the phase is a minimum s-t cut, meaning its weight is less than or equal to this cut. Thus, the cut of the phase is optimal and is a global minimum cut.

Case 2: No minimum cut separates s from t

This implies that every minimum cut places s and t on the same side. By merging s and t, we only eliminate cuts that separate them. Consequently, the merged graph retains a cut of weight k. The remaining phases correspond exactly to running the algorithm on this smaller graph. By our inductive hypothesis, one of these subsequent phases will successfully find a cut of weight k.

Since every cut of the phase represents a valid cut of the original input graph, no cut can have a weight strictly less than k. Therefore, the smallest cut across all phases has a weight of exactly k, proving it is a minimum cut of the graph.

Time and Space Complexity

With Fibonacci heaps, the Stoer-Wagner algorithm runs in O(|V||E| + |V|² log |V|) time, which matched the fastest algorithm known when it was published. The space complexity is O(|V|²) to store the dense adjacency matrix required for rapid weight updates, or O(|V| + |E|) if utilising adjacency lists, alongside O(|V|) auxiliary space for the priority queue and vertex tracking arrays.
